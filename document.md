# PRSIM  
## Minimum Information. Maximum Relevance.

> “As motivation and/or ability to process arguments is decreased, peripheral cues become relatively more important determinants of persuasion.”  
> — Richard E. Petty & John T. Cacioppo, 1986

## The problem

Most ecommerce product pages are designed around the **product**, not around the **decision**.

A conventional Shopify page tries to answer every possible question for every possible buyer. It accumulates benefits, specifications, lifestyle images, reviews, comparison tables, guarantees, delivery messages, FAQs, recommendations, pop-ups, badges, and repeated calls to action.

This creates a paradox:

> The decisive argument may already be on the page—and still remain unseen because it is competing with everything else.

A buyer looking for a cabin bag compatible with Ryanair does not need to process the full history of the brand, every material option, reviews from business commuters, cycling use cases, or compatibility with twenty unrelated airlines. The buyer first needs one answer:

**Will this bag fit under a Ryanair seat without an extra fee?**

Every irrelevant element creates another object to notice, classify, reject, remember, or compare. The user is forced to perform the filtering work that the interface should have performed first.

PRSIM reverses that model.

---

## The theoretical thread: Petty and Cacioppo

The central framework behind PRSIM is the **Elaboration Likelihood Model** developed by Richard E. Petty and John T. Cacioppo.

The model describes persuasion along a continuum of elaboration:

- Under **high elaboration**, people carefully examine the quality and relevance of the arguments. This is commonly described as the **central route**.
- Under **low elaboration**, judgments depend more heavily on simple associations, heuristics, source cues, familiarity, attractiveness, repetition, or other **peripheral cues**.

The route is influenced by two essential conditions:

1. **Motivation** — does the person care enough to think?
2. **Ability** — does the person have the cognitive opportunity and capacity to think?

PRSIM focuses especially on the second condition.

A motivated buyer can still be prevented from evaluating the strongest product arguments when the page fragments attention across too many competing elements. Distraction, complexity, uncertainty, time pressure, and irrelevant information can all reduce the practical ability to elaborate.

This does **not** mean that visual clutter mechanically flips a neurological switch from “central” to “peripheral” processing. The ELM is a continuum, not a binary brain mode. The more defensible claim is:

> Page noise can reduce argument scrutiny, making the quality of the decisive product arguments less likely to determine the decision.

In Petty, Cacioppo, and Schumann’s advertising research, argument quality had a stronger effect under high-involvement conditions, while a celebrity endorser had more influence under low-involvement conditions. Earlier work by Petty, Wells, and Brock also showed that distraction can disrupt message-relevant thinking.

For commerce, the implication is clear: a page should protect the buyer’s ability to evaluate the arguments that genuinely determine product fit.

## Attention is a competitive resource

Attention is not allocated equally to everything visible on a page.

Headlines, badges, images, prices, reviews, navigation, promotional banners, chat widgets, cross-sells, color selectors, and delivery messages compete for selection. Working memory must then hold and combine the selected information long enough to support a decision.

Research on perceptual load, cognitive load, distraction, and consumer information load all supports the broader idea that human information processing has limits. Importantly, classic consumer research found that adding product information could make people feel more satisfied while simultaneously producing poorer purchase decisions.

The ecommerce problem is therefore not simply “too much content.” It is a sequence of hidden cognitive costs:

```text
Notice → Interpret → Filter → Retain → Compare → Decide
```

Every decision-irrelevant module adds work somewhere in that chain.

This is the **clutter tax**: the strongest argument loses part of its persuasive power because the buyer must first find it, recognize its relevance, and separate it from noise.

---

## The PRSIM principle

“Less is more” is too simplistic.

A minimal page can fail when it removes an essential answer, proof, reassurance, or constraint. PRSIM is not minimalism for aesthetic reasons.

Its principle is:

> **Less irrelevant information is more.**

Or, in product terms:

> **Minimum information. Maximum relevance.**

PRSIM does not ask, “What can we say about this product?”

It asks:

> “What must this buyer understand, believe, and resolve to make this decision now?”

## From product page to decision interface

### Conventional ecommerce

```text
One product
    ↓
All available information
    ↓
One exhaustive page
    ↓
The buyer filters it
    ↓
Decision
```

### PRSIM

```text
Product truth
      +
Buyer context
      +
Purchase intent
      ↓
Relevance ranking
      ↓
Selected arguments
      ↓
Selected proof
      ↓
Adaptive page sequence
      ↓
Decision
```

The SKU remains the same. What changes is the **selection, order, depth, format, proof, and call to action**.

PRSIM can use context such as:

- purchase for self or as a gift;
- destination and expected conditions;
- airline, train, bicycle, car, or walking;
- departure date or birthday deadline;
- budget and price sensitivity;
- trip duration;
- novice or expert knowledge;
- primary fear or objection;
- device, traffic source, and search intent;
- previous product-page behavior;
- preferred form of proof.

Demographics should only influence the page when they are genuinely decision-relevant. “Age 18” is weak information by itself. “First Ryanair trip, limited budget, Dublin in six days, rain expected” is actionable context.

## A simple page grammar

A PRSIM page can remain structurally simple:

### Hero — Answer the primary decision question

The hero should communicate the most decisive proposition, not the brand’s complete value story.

```text
✓ Fits under a Ryanair seat
40 × 20 × 25 cm
No cabin-bag supplement
[Travel without the extra fee]
```

### Section 1 — Present the strongest relevant argument

One section, one idea.

```text
The bag costs €89 once.
A cabin-bag fee can be paid repeatedly.
```

### Section 2 — Prove it

The proof format should match the claim:

- dimensional diagram for airline compatibility;
- delivery timeline for a deadline;
- rain test for water resistance;
- packing visual for capacity;
- comparison table for an analytical buyer;
- contextual review for social proof.

### Section 3 — Remove the remaining risk

Show only the reassurance that blocks this purchase:

- delivery before the birthday;
- free exchange for a gift;
- warranty for a cautious buyer;
- laptop protection for a digital nomad;
- comfort proof for an older traveler;
- verified peer review for a first-time buyer.

### CTA — Express the user’s objective

The action can be framed around the intended outcome:

```text
[Receive it before Friday]
[Give this bag]
[Travel without the baggage fee]
[Protect my laptop]
[Order for today]
```

## The same bag, five different decisions

### Student flying Ryanair to rainy Dublin

Show:

1. Ryanair compatibility;
2. baggage-fee savings;
3. rain protection;
4. delivery before departure;
5. review from a young low-cost traveler.

Do not foreground premium materials, business organization, or a twenty-airline table.

### Parent buying a birthday gift for a Japan trip

Show:

1. arrival before the birthday;
2. suitability for the trip;
3. easy exchange;
4. packing capacity;
5. reviews from other gift buyers.

Do not lead with technical fabric specifications.

### Premium Eurostar business traveler

Show:

1. office-to-train use;
2. laptop and document organization;
3. compact premium silhouette;
4. material quality;
5. discreet purchase CTA.

Do not lead with discounts or youth-oriented social content.

### Analytical engineer comparing products

Show:

1. exact dimensions, capacity, and weight;
2. comparison table;
3. material and stress-test data;
4. warranty;
5. detailed verified review.

Here, PRSIM may display **more** information because that information is relevant to the user’s decision process.

### Traveler leaving tomorrow

Show:

1. in-stock status;
2. exact delivery time;
3. three essential specifications;
4. express checkout.

Then stop.

## What PRSIM is—and is not

PRSIM is:

- a **relevance engine**;
- an **attention-allocation system**;
- an **adaptive information architecture**;
- a **decision interface**;
- a method for protecting strong arguments from irrelevant competition.

PRSIM is not:

- personalization for decoration;
- inserting a first name into generic copy;
- showing more recommendations;
- hiding inconvenient product facts;
- using false urgency or manufactured scarcity;
- assuming demographics explain intent;
- removing information merely to make a page look elegant.

A useful distinction is:

```text
Traditional personalization:
Who is most likely to buy this product?

PRSIM:
Which truths about this product matter to this decision?
```

---

## Ethical and product safeguards

Relevance filtering must never become selective deception.

PRSIM should always:

1. show any fact that could materially disqualify the product;
2. preserve access to complete specifications;
3. avoid hiding fees, limitations, incompatibilities, or return conditions;
4. distinguish known context from inferred context;
5. allow the buyer to correct an incorrect assumption;
6. use genuine reviews and verifiable proof;
7. optimize informed confidence, not accidental compliance.

The goal is not to make the user know less.

The goal is to make the user encounter the **right knowledge first**.

---

## The product hypothesis

PRSIM’s core hypothesis can be stated precisely:

> Holding the product and purchase intent constant, reducing decision-irrelevant information while increasing the salience of decision-relevant information should improve comprehension, confidence, and purchase propensity.

This remains an empirical product hypothesis. The underlying research supports its mechanisms, but it does not by itself prove that every adaptive ecommerce page will convert better.

A rigorous test should compare:

```text
A — Exhaustive conventional Shopify page
B — Static minimalist page
C — PRSIM context-selected page
```

Measure:

- recognition of the decisive product attributes;
- recall of key arguments;
- time required to establish product fit;
- decision confidence;
- add-to-cart rate;
- purchase conversion;
- support questions;
- cancellation, return, and regret rates.

The expected result is not simply faster conversion. It is a decision that is **faster because it is clearer**, and more durable because it is grounded in the product arguments that actually matter.

---

## Why the name PRSIM

PRSIM evokes a **prism**: an object that separates an undifferentiated input into meaningful components.

PRSIM does the same with product information.

```text
Hundreds of product facts
          ↓
        PRSIM
          ↓
A small set of decision-relevant signals
```

The altered spelling makes the name itself slightly incomplete and unexpected. The eye is invited to reconstruct the familiar word while noticing only what is required to recover its meaning.

That is the brand idea in miniature:

> **Remove the noise. Preserve the signal. Direct attention to what matters.**
